Neuroflorescence

A brain made of light that never stops changing. It grows, learns, forgets, is hurt and repairs itself, live and never the same twice.

The idea

For a long time the adult brain was thought of as fixed wiring. We now know that it keeps changing for as long as we live. What we practise grows stronger, what we stop using fades, and after an injury the brain can find new ways around the damage. This ability is called neuroplasticity.

Neuroflorescence does not explain this. It lets you watch it happen. A brain seen from the side is a flowing, folded tissue of coloured light. Currents condense out of it where sparks of experience keep arriving from outside, and dissolve again when the experience stops. Now and then a small region goes dark, and the currents slowly learn to flow around it. There are no words on the screen, no chapters and no loop. Someone passing by sees a living brain in a few seconds. Someone who sits on the steps for ten minutes sees habits form, fade and reroute.

The name joins neuro with two words that sound almost alike: florescence, the time when a plant comes into flower, and fluorescence, the glow some substances give off when light falls on them, which neuroscientists use to watch living neurons at work. Here, pathways come into flower where experience keeps arriving, glow while they are used, and wilt back into the tissue when they are not.

The piece asks a quiet question that runs through the BRAIN theme: if every experience reshapes the organ that makes us who we are, how much of who we are is made by what happens to us?

What you may notice

  • Tissue. The drifting, folded colour that fills the brain: countless neurons and their possible connections. Some of it spills out in slow plumes and mixes with the darkness around the brain.
  • Bursts. Blooms of light where new connections sprout: many in a young brain, and again around an injury.
  • Experience. Small sparks drifting in from the darkness outside the brain. Wherever they keep arriving, a pathway is being practised.
  • Pathways. Glowing currents between two regions. Each signal that gets through makes its pathway stronger, so it gathers more light. When the sparks stop coming, it fades back into the tissue.
  • Injury. A small region slowly goes dark, with a softly glowing, cracked border. Signals stop at its edge until the pathway grows a detour, and over minutes the damage settles into a faint scar.

The rhythm of a lifetime

Nothing on the screen is scheduled. Pathways are born, practised, abandoned and pruned on their own; injuries happen at random and heal. What changes slowly is the character of the brain, over a "lifetime" of about 24 minutes:

Young. Bursts everywhere, many faint pathways, restless tissue.
Growing up. Pruning sets in. The tissue around the pathways dims, and the practised routes stand out.
Adult. Habits shift. Old pathways fade while new ones are learned, and now and then an injury and its recovery.
Late. Calmer and sparser, with fewer new beginnings.

At the end the brain dissolves into darkness and a new one begins to grow, different from the last.

The science underneath

Growth. In early childhood the brain forms synapses far faster than it will ever need. In parts of the human cortex, synapse density peaks in the first years of life at levels well above those of adults (Huttenlocher, 1979; Huttenlocher & Dabholkar, 1997).

Fire together, wire together. Donald Hebb proposed in 1949 that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the link between them grows stronger. Long-term potentiation, discovered by Bliss and Lømo (1973), is a measurable form of this strengthening and a basis for learning and memory.

Use it or lose it. Synaptic pruning removes a large share of early connections during childhood and adolescence. Activity helps decide which synapses stay and which go.

Injury and recovery. A stroke cuts the pathways that cross the damaged tissue. Surviving neurons can sprout new connections, and nearby areas can take over lost functions. Nudo and colleagues (1996) showed that rehabilitative training after a small cortical stroke shapes how the surrounding cortex reorganises.

A lifetime of change. Plasticity does not stop in childhood. Learning to juggle changes grey matter within months (Draganski et al., 2004), and London taxi drivers who memorise the city show changes in the hippocampus (Maguire et al., 2000).

A note on simplification. This is an artwork, not a biophysical model. Real neurons number in the billions, and plasticity also involves chemistry, new neurons, glial cells and myelin. The piece keeps a handful of principles that can be seen at a glance.

Care and sensitivity

The piece is calm by design. It has no flashing or strobing, and brightness changes are slow and local. Injury is shown as quiet darkening followed by repair, never as violence, out of respect for visitors who live with brain injury, stroke or neurological conditions. It needs no reading, no sound and no prior knowledge, so it is open to every age and language.

How it works

The piece runs entirely on the graphics card (WebGL 2). Five passes repeat every frame:

1. Flow. A small texture stores how the tissue moves at every point: slowly changing swirls (curl noise), currents along each pathway whose strength depends on how well it has been learned, and a gentle withdrawal from an injury.
2. Particles. About 262,000 particles are moved by that flow. A third of them are drawn toward the pathways. Particles are reborn at random places in the brain, or in the centre of a burst. Half of them may wander: where slow plumes rise, they leave the brain and drift out into the screen, fading the further they go.
3. Smearing. The previous frame is shifted slightly along the flow and faded. This turns moving points into fibres, streaks and marbled colour.
4. Drawing. The particles are added on top as tiny points of light.
5. Light. The image is lit as a relief, which gives the tissue its sculpted surface. Bloom, the glowing regions, the signals, the sparks of experience and the injury border are added last.

The learning happens in a small model on the CPU (brain.js). Six slots hold pathways between regions of the cortex. Each pathway sprouts, may be practised for a while, then is neglected and pruned, and its slot is reused. Every signal that reaches the end of its pathway strengthens it. An injury cuts the pathways that cross it, and their points then migrate around the damage, where the detour has to be learned again. The lifetime keyframes in life.js only change how strongly these rules act.

Running it on site

The piece is built for the playback chain the open call describes, as it is. No hardware changes are needed, and nothing has to be installed besides a current web browser.

ScreenNEC LED wall, 3840 × 3024 px (80:63), 20′3″ × 16′2″
Playback PCWindows, Intel Core i9-10850K, 32 GB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 (24 GB)
SignalOne full-screen output through the existing Analog Way EKS-4K switcher
SoftwareChrome or Edge. No network, server, build step, audio or input devices.

Set-up

  1. Copy the folder to the PC, for example to C:\Neuroflorescence. It holds only static files and runs straight from disk.
  2. Set the output that feeds the wall to 3840 × 3024. With ?res=4k the piece always renders at that size and fits itself to the window, keeping the 80:63 shape.
  3. Start it full screen in kiosk mode with Chrome: 
    "C:\Program Files\Google\Chrome\Application\chrome.exe" --kiosk --no-first-run --noerrdialogs "file:///C:/Neuroflorescence/index.html?ui=0&res=4k"
    or with Edge: 
    "C:\Program Files (x86)\Microsoft\Edge\Application\msedge.exe" --kiosk "file:///C:/Neuroflorescence/index.html?ui=0&res=4k" --edge-kiosk-type=fullscreen
  4. Start it at boot with a Windows Task Scheduler task that runs the command above when an automatically signed-in account logs on.
  5. Keep the PC awake. In the Windows power settings, set sleep, display off and the screen saver to never, and schedule Windows Update restarts outside opening hours.

Unattended operation

Once started, the piece needs no attention. It plays without end and never repeats: each lifetime of about 24 minutes is followed by a new one. If the graphics driver resets or an error ever stops the animation, the page reloads itself within a few seconds. It depends on no network, clock or outside service, so restarting the PC always brings it back.

Checks on the first day

  • Motion is smooth. The piece is designed for 60 frames per second.
  • The darkest tissue shows on the wall without banding. The piece lives in deep darks, so the wall's brightness and gamma may need a look with the AV team.
  • If the PC has room to spare, a sharper image is one address change away: &sim=2560&particles=768 (see the options below).

URL options

?ui=0Kiosk mode: no buttons, no cursor. If an error ever stops the animation, the page reloads itself.
?res=4kAlways render at 3840 × 3024, whatever the window size.
?label=1Show a small title label in the lower left corner.
?seed=7Start from a specific variation.
?start=600Jump to a point in the lifetime, in seconds.
?life=600Length of a lifetime in seconds (default 1440).
?speed=2Play faster, useful for previewing.
?particles=512Size of the particle grid: 512 gives 262,144 particles (default), 256 gives 65,536, 1024 about a million.
?sim=1920Width of the internal simulation image (default 1920). Higher is sharper and slower.

Options can be combined, for example index.html?ui=0&res=4k&sim=2560&particles=768.
