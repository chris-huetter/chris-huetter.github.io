Terra Circadia

a protein landscape that keeps time

Your brain runs on proteins, and proteins work in teams. Terra Circadia lays nearly 10,000 human proteins out as a landscape: where they crowd together the land rises, and the teams behind everyday abilities gather into mountains. At its heart is the body clock, and the piece keeps the real time of day.

Enter the piece → Full window, no controls

The idea

Almost every job in a cell is done by proteins, tiny machines that touch and work together. Mapped by who touches whom, the human proteins form one vast network. Drawn as dots and lines it is a tangle no one can read. Terra Circadia turns it into land. Every protein lifts the ground a little where it sits, so busy neighborhoods become hills. A team of proteins with one job, such as keeping time, learning or remembering, gathers in one place and rises into a mountain, and the proteins that belong to two teams form the ridge between their mountains.

The piece is built around the body clock: 83 proteins that keep a rhythm of about 24 hours in your cells and tell your body when to wake up and when to sleep. Terra Circadia reads the real clock, so the team that rises beside the body clock depends on the hour of your visit.

How to read the map

Six things to look for. The same key sits in the lower left corner of the wall.

A pointOne protein: a tiny machine inside your cells.
A lineTwo proteins that touch and work together.
A hillA crowd of proteins working together; the bigger the crowd, the higher the hill.
A colored mountainA team of proteins with one job, such as keeping time or learning.
Points and lines above a mountainWho works with whom inside that team.
GreenProteins that are members of two teams at once.

Each team keeps its color through the piece: the body clock gold, moving apricot, being social blue, learning pink, remembering violet, rewiring sky blue. The land is one deep blue across the whole wall. There is no red.

Six scenes

Each scene shows one idea and one visible change. Every scene stands on its own, so a visitor can arrive at any moment; together they take about three and a half minutes, and then begin again.

1

Now

40 s

“Inside you, thousands of proteins work together.”

The whole network floats as a cloud of points and lines, then settles onto the ground, and the land rises out of it. Notes point out one protein (CLOCK), one link, and CLOCK’s cluster: 15 partners, 10 of which also keep time.

2

Body clock

28 s

“Your body runs on a 24-hour rhythm.”

The 83 body clock proteins light up where they are, then gather into one gold mountain; the network of who works with whom floats above it.

3

The loop

30 s

“Two groups take turns, like a seesaw.”

Six starters and eight stoppers rise as two summits that take turns. The group at work rises and glows, its proteins larger and brighter; the resting group sinks and dims. A dial turns once for every turn of the loop, which takes about a day.

4

This hour

28 s

“Good morning.” · “Lunchtime.” · “After lunch.” · “Late afternoon.”

The body clock beside the team for this time of day. The line says how many proteins they share; those proteins form a green ridge, and the two with the most links are named.

5

Tonight

28 s

“Sleep helps your brain rewire.”

The rewiring team (184 proteins that strengthen, build and prune the links between nerve cells) beside the body clock, joined by the 5 proteins they share.

6

Your brain

48 s

“While you watched, your brain was rewiring.”

Teams rise one after another in new places, link up with the mountains already standing, and sink back into the land; never more than three at once. A caption counts what they share. Data credits close the loop.

The hour decides

Terra Circadia follows the clock of the computer it runs on. In “This hour” the body clock is paired with a different team as the day goes on, a small story of a day at the museum. The last scene says when the land will change next: “Come back at 11 am: the land will look different.”

A way in, not a timetable

Pairing hours with teams is a storytelling choice: it gives each visit its own view of the data. It does not claim that a team works only at that hour. What the scene shows (the size of each team and the proteins two teams share) comes straight from the data, and the piece counts it afresh every time it starts.

The body clock’s loop

In each cell a small set of proteins keeps the clock going. Terra Circadia shows them as two groups:

The startersCLOCK, BMAL1, NPAS2, BMAL2, RORA, RORC. When they are busy, they tell the cell to make the stoppers.
The stoppersPER1–3, CRY1–2, NR1D1–2, TIMELESS. They pile up slowly; when there are enough, they switch the starters off, then wear away.

Then the starters are free again and the next turn begins. One turn takes about a day. On the wall a turn is compressed into nine seconds: the group at work rises and glows, while the resting group sinks to about a third of its height and dims, and the dial’s hand goes round once.

Using the piece

Terra Circadia is made for the two-story digital screen in the Lee Family Exchange at the MIT Museum: an NEC LED wall of 3840 × 3024 pixels (80:63), 20′ 3″ × 16′ 2″, seen from stadium seating. It reads from across the room, and its notes reward a closer look. It fills any screen edge to edge, whatever its shape (80:63, 3:2, 16:9), and runs in any current desktop browser. Left alone, it plays forever.

Reading the screen

  • HeadlineTop left: the scene’s one idea, typed out, with a line of detail beneath it. The detail dims after a while, so the map’s own labels read clearly.
  • LabelsThe name and size of each mountain; the network above a mountain shows exactly that many proteins. A label sits above its floating network (in “Body clock”, to its right). When a tall mountain stands at the back of the land, its label moves beside the network so it stays on the wall, clear of the headline and of other labels.
  • NotesBoxes at the sides, each with a thin line to the protein or spot it explains, for example a protein’s full name and the two teams it belongs to. Box and line fade in and out together.
  • KeyLower left: how to read the map.
  • DialIn “The loop”: one turn of the clock, about 24 hours.
  • CaptionIn “Your brain”: which team rose, what it shares with the mountains already standing, and which team settled back.

What you will notice

  • The land fills the wall: it has no edge, runs past the sides and fades into the blue in the distance. It sways gently from side to side.
  • A network that drifts behind the headline dims while it is there, so the words stay readable.
  • Proteins drift gently around their places and move at their own pace, so mountains rise and sink softly rather than switching.
  • Words type themselves out in place. Nothing jumps; the space for every line is kept from the start.
  • Nothing flashes (well under three flashes a second), and the palette stays calm.

How it’s made

The piece is a single web page: one JavaScript module (src/landscape.js) drawing with WebGL 2 through three.js, bundled with Vite. Everything it needs, including the data and the typefaces, ships with it, so it runs offline.

Protein network→Map layout→Height map→Contour lines→Teams rise→Networks float→Words
  1. Data. 9,830 human proteins and 85,576 physical interactions from STRING v12.0 (combined score 700 or higher, the largest connected part). Teams from the Gene Ontology (release 2026-07-26): proteins tied to a biological process by experiment or curation, including its more specific terms, kept only if they are on the network.
  2. Map layout. When the piece starts, a force layout in two dimensions (springs with a short rest length, plus sampled repulsion) runs for 140 steps and places proteins that work together near each other. The result is fitted into a circle in the middle of the land.
  3. Height map. Every protein lays a soft disc of height where it sits into a 768 × 616 texture; a team’s proteins lay much more, so a team gathered in one place becomes a mountain. Heights round off softly (1 − e−kx), so mountains swell instead of spiking. The map is redrawn every frame, so the land rises and sinks smoothly as proteins move.
  4. Contour lines. The land is a fine mesh lifted by the height map under fixed light. Lines follow every 1/60 of the height, every fifth one stronger, about a pixel wide at any size; where they would crowd at a grazing angle they fade out. Low land carries a faint survey grid. Beyond the proteins the land lies flat, reaches past every edge of the wall and fades into the same blue far away.
  5. Teams rise. Each scene says which teams gather where. Each protein eases toward its place at its own pace and drifts gently around it; labels follow the measured summits, read back from the height map four times a second.
  6. Networks float. Above each mountain, its team’s links form a slowly turning ball. A protein’s longitude on the ball is its direction from the team’s center on the map and its latitude its distance, so neighbors on the map stay neighbors on the ball. Longer links bundle together; in the last scene, links between two teams arch over the valley as one bridge.
  7. Rendering. Everything is drawn into a high-range buffer and tone-mapped (1 − e−x), so dense, glowing areas build softly toward white instead of clipping. Words and leader lines are HTML and SVG over the picture.

Adapting to the screen

The picture fills its screen edge to edge, whatever the shape, with no frame or letterbox. The land has no border, so a wider screen simply shows more of it at the sides, while the height of the view stays the same and the mountains keep room for their networks above them. It renders at the screen’s own pixel density, up to as many pixels as the Exchange wall has (3840 × 3024). Every text size follows the screen’s height.

Run it on your own computer

You need Node.js 20 or newer and Git.

git clone https://github.com/chris-huetter/BRAIN-TerraCircadia.git
cd BRAIN-TerraCircadia
npm ci
npm run dev

Then open http://localhost:5173/. The protein data is already included, so nothing else needs to be downloaded. For stills at the wall’s size:

npx playwright install chromium   # once
npm run capture                   # stills at 3840 × 3024 into ./captures

Install it on the museum wall

The piece is built for the existing playback PC (Windows, RTX 3090). No hardware changes are needed; it sends one full-screen signal at 3840 × 3024 through the existing switcher.

  1. Build the piece once: npm ci, then npm run build. The finished piece is in the dist folder.
  2. Serve that folder on the PC itself, for example with npx vite preview --port 4173.
  3. Open it full screen in Chrome’s kiosk mode: 
    "C:\Program Files\Google\Chrome\Application\chrome.exe" ^
  --kiosk --start-fullscreen "http://localhost:4173/?wall"
  4. Set the PC’s clock to Boston time (Eastern Time); the piece follows it.
  5. To start at boot, put both commands in a Windows Task Scheduler task that runs at log-on.

We will agree the final setup (start-up, automatic restart, and a pre-rendered video loop as a fallback) with the museum’s AV team.

Notes on the science

The landscape shows only what the data shows: which proteins physically touch, with high confidence (STRING), and which proteins curators have tied to a process by experiment or careful review (Gene Ontology). Each team is one or more Gene Ontology processes, named in plain words:

TeamGene Ontology process
Body clockcircadian rhythm (GO:0007623)
Movinglocomotory behavior (GO:0007626)
Learninglearning (GO:0007612)
Rememberingmemory (GO:0007613)
Being socialsocial behavior (GO:0035176)
Rewiringregulation of synaptic plasticity (GO:0048167), long-term synaptic potentiation (GO:0060291), synapse assembly (GO:0007416), synapse pruning (GO:0098883)

The loop is simplified from the textbook model of the clock: in the cell, the RORs and the REV-ERBs (NR1D1 and NR1D2) act mainly on BMAL1 in a second, smaller loop, and the real cycle involves many more proteins and steps. The map is an artistic interpretation, not a clinical tool. The story is about what brains do: the screen names no diseases, and protein names that contain a disease word are left out of the data on purpose.

Two ideas from network science shape the piece. Reading a protein network like a map, where position carries meaning, builds on network cartographs (Hütter et al., 2022). And that proteins with a shared role gather in one neighborhood of the network, where neighborhoods that overlap share biology, is a central finding of network medicine (Menche et al., 2015): it is what the mountains and the ridges between them show.
